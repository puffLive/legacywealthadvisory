A Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) is one of the most familiar savings tools in Canada, yet many people are not sure how much they are allowed to put in. Your contribution room is personal. It depends on your income, your pension, and how much room you have left over from earlier years.

## How your RRSP room is calculated

For each year, new RRSP room is generally the **lower** of:

- 18% of your earned income from the previous year, or
- the annual dollar limit set by the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA).

For the 2026 tax year, the RRSP dollar limit is **$33,810**. To reach that limit, your 2025 earned income would need to be roughly $188,000 or more.

If you belong to a workplace pension plan or a deferred profit sharing plan, your new room is reduced by a **pension adjustment**. This keeps people with workplace pensions and people without them on a similar footing.

## Unused room carries forward

Room you do not use is not lost. Unused contribution room carries forward indefinitely, so a person who contributed little in their early working years may have a large amount of room available later, when income and cash flow are higher.

## Where to find your number

The most reliable figure is your **RRSP deduction limit**, shown on:

- your most recent Notice of Assessment from the CRA, and
- your CRA My Account online.

This number already includes your carried-forward room and any pension adjustment.

## The deadline for 2026 contributions

Contributions made during 2026, and in the first 60 days of 2027, can be deducted on your 2026 tax return. For the 2026 tax year, the deadline is **March 1, 2027**.

You do not have to claim the deduction in the year you contribute. Some people contribute now and save the deduction for a future year when they expect to be in a higher tax bracket.

## Be careful about over-contributing

The CRA allows a lifetime over-contribution cushion of **$2,000**. Beyond that, excess contributions are generally subject to a tax of **1% per month** until they are withdrawn or new room becomes available. Checking your deduction limit before contributing helps you avoid this.

## Questions worth asking before you contribute

- Is an RRSP the right account for this money, or would a TFSA or FHSA fit better?
- Would a **spousal RRSP** help balance retirement income between you and your partner?
- Should you take the deduction this year, or carry it forward?
- How does this contribution fit with your retirement income and estate plans?

The right answer depends on your income today, your expected income in retirement, and your family's goals. A planner can help you look at the whole picture rather than one account at a time.
