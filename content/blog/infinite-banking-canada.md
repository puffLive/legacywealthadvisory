The idea of **“becoming your own banker”** has attracted attention among Canadians looking for greater control over their money. Often called *infinite banking*, the strategy generally involves using the cash value of a permanent life insurance policy as a potential source of financing.

But it is important to separate the concept from the marketing. **Infinite banking does not make you a bank, create interest-free borrowing or guarantee investment returns.** Whether it makes financial sense depends on your insurance needs, cash flow, borrowing costs and alternatives.

## What is infinite banking?

Infinite banking is a planning concept commonly associated with **participating whole life insurance**. These policies provide permanent life insurance protection and contractual cash values. They may also receive policyowner dividends, which are **not guaranteed**.

As cash value accumulates, a policyholder may have options to access liquidity through an insurer policy loan or a separate loan from a financial institution secured by the policy. These are different arrangements with different contractual and tax consequences.

## How does it work?

1. **Establish a suitable permanent insurance policy.** A participating whole life policy is selected based on a genuine long-term insurance need, affordability and policy features.
2. **Build cash value over time.** The policy has guaranteed values under its contract, while dividends and related illustrated values may vary. Early cash values can be significantly lower than cumulative premiums.
3. **Consider borrowing options.** The policyholder may request an insurer policy loan or apply to a third-party lender using the policy as collateral, subject to the terms and available lending value.
4. **Use borrowed funds for a defined purpose.** Examples could include business liquidity or another planned expense. Borrowing still creates a debt and interest costs.
5. **Manage repayment and policy sustainability.** Outstanding loans and interest can reduce the net death benefit or, in some circumstances, jeopardize coverage.

### A simplified example

Suppose a business owner has a participating whole life policy with sufficient available value and is considering borrowing **$40,000** for a business expense. Before borrowing, they should compare the loan's interest rate, repayment terms, policy impact, tax treatment and alternative financing costs.

The $40,000 is **borrowed money, not an investment return or a withdrawal of $40,000 in profits**. A policy illustration and lender terms are needed to assess what is actually available.

## Potential advantages

### Permanent life insurance protection

For someone who needs lifelong coverage, a participating whole life policy can provide a contractual death benefit and cash values, provided the policy remains in force and its conditions are met.

### Another potential source of liquidity

Policy value may support borrowing without immediately selling other assets. Access is not unlimited, and lenders can impose conditions.

### Long-term planning flexibility

Certain exempt life insurance policies can accumulate value on a tax-advantaged basis under Canadian rules. This does **not** mean every loan or withdrawal is tax-free.

### Estate and business planning

Permanent insurance can sometimes support estate liquidity, business continuity or succession objectives. Corporate ownership introduces additional legal, tax and accounting considerations.

## The risks and costs are real

- **Premium commitments:** Whole life insurance typically costs more initially than comparable term insurance and is generally a long-term commitment.
- **Early-year cash values:** Cash value may be substantially below the premiums paid in the first several years.
- **Loan interest:** Insurer loans and third-party collateral loans charge interest; borrowing rates and conditions can change.
- **Non-guaranteed dividends:** Participating dividends are determined by the insurer and can change. Illustrated dividend growth is not guaranteed.
- **Reduced benefits or lapse risk:** Outstanding loans and accrued interest can reduce death benefits, and excessive borrowing may threaten the policy.
- **Tax consequences:** An insurer policy loan can constitute a disposition for Canadian tax purposes and may trigger taxable income depending on the policy's adjusted cost basis and other factors. Withdrawals can also be taxable. A third-party collateral loan is structured differently, but tax treatment still requires individual review.
- **Opportunity cost:** Money committed to premiums is money that cannot be used elsewhere. The strategy should be compared against savings, investments, debt repayment and conventional financing.

## Is infinite banking better than an RRSP or TFSA?

These tools serve different purposes. An **RRSP** is generally designed for tax-deferred retirement savings; a **TFSA** allows eligible tax-free investment growth and withdrawals; and participating whole life insurance primarily provides permanent insurance with cash-value features.

A life insurance borrowing strategy should not automatically replace registered savings, an emergency fund or a diversified portfolio. The right mix depends on your goals and financial position.

## Who might consider the strategy?

It may be worth evaluating for someone who has a genuine need for permanent insurance, stable long-term cash flow, sufficient liquidity outside the policy and a specific reason to access capital in the future.

It may be less appropriate for someone who needs affordable temporary insurance, has high-interest consumer debt, limited emergency savings or expects to need their money back in the near term.

## Five questions to ask before committing

1. Do I need permanent life insurance independently of the borrowing concept?
2. What are the **guaranteed** cash values, and what depends on **non-guaranteed** dividends?
3. How much can I actually borrow, when, and at what interest rate?
4. What happens to the policy and death benefit if I do not repay?
5. How does this compare with term insurance, RRSPs, TFSAs and conventional borrowing?
