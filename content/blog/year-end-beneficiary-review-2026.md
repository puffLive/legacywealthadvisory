# Year-End Beneficiary Review: Are Your Accounts and Insurance Still Aligned?

As the year draws to a close, many Canadians review investment returns, contributions and tax receipts. But one important part of financial planning is easier to overlook: **who receives your assets when you die?**

A beneficiary designation made years ago may no longer reflect your family circumstances or estate-planning intentions.

## 1. Start with the actual records

Make a list of your life insurance policies, RRSPs, RRIFs, TFSAs and workplace benefits. Check the beneficiary information held by each insurer, financial institution or plan administrator. Do not assume that updating a will automatically changes every beneficiary designation.

Rules differ by product, province and plan. Where there is a conflict or uncertainty, obtain legal advice before making changes.

## 2. Understand that registered accounts are not all treated alike

An RRSP or RRIF can have important income-tax consequences at death. A spouse or common-law partner may qualify for special rollover treatment in certain circumstances, but it is not automatic in every case.

A TFSA has different rules. A spouse or common-law partner who is properly designated as a **successor holder** may be able to continue the TFSA after the holder's death. A beneficiary designation is not necessarily equivalent to a successor-holder designation. Provincial law and the terms of the arrangement matter.

## 3. Revisit major life changes

Marriage, separation, divorce, the birth of a child, the death of a beneficiary, a business sale or a change in financial dependants can all be reasons to revisit your designations. Also consider whether you have named a contingent beneficiary where the provider permits one.

## 4. Coordinate insurance with the broader estate plan

Life insurance may be intended to provide liquidity for dependants, support a business succession arrangement or help equalize inheritances. A beneficiary choice should fit that purpose and be coordinated with the will, powers of attorney and any relevant trust or shareholder agreements.

## 5. Ask three questions before year-end

- Are the people named on my accounts and policies still the people I intend to benefit?
- Do my beneficiary designations and estate documents work together?
- Could the tax consequences or timing of payments create an unintended result?

A year-end review does not necessarily mean changing anything. It means checking that your instructions still reflect your wishes.
