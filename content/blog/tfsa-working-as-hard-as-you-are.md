Many Canadians have a Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA), but having one and using it effectively are two different things.

As we approach 2027, consider whether your TFSA is aligned with your broader financial objectives.

## 1. Understand your contribution room

The 2026 annual TFSA dollar limit is $7,000. Unused room carries forward, and excess contributions generally attract a 1% monthly tax on the excess. Reconcile your contribution records across financial institutions before contributing, as CRA records may lag.

## 2. Think beyond traditional savings

A TFSA can hold qualified investments such as mutual funds, eligible stocks and bonds, and guaranteed investment certificates. The appropriate approach depends on your objectives, risk tolerance and time horizon. Contributions are not deductible, but investment income and withdrawals are generally tax-free in Canada.

## 3. Understand the power of long-term growth

Consider an illustration of $7,000 contributed at each year-end with a constant hypothetical annual return of 6%.

| Years | Contributions | Hypothetical balance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | $35,000 | $39,460 |
| 10 | $70,000 | $92,266 |
| 15 | $105,000 | $162,932 |
| 20 | $140,000 | $257,499 |

This assumes sufficient contribution room, reinvested returns, no withdrawals and no fees. Actual returns fluctuate and may be negative. Future annual limits may differ. This is not a forecast or recommendation.

## 4. Be strategic about withdrawals

Amounts withdrawn in 2026 are generally added back to contribution room on January 1, 2027. Replacing withdrawn money during 2026 without sufficient unused room could lead to excess contribution taxes.

## 5. Give every investment a purpose

A TFSA could support retirement, emergency savings, major purchases or long-term financial independence. The time horizon and need for liquidity should inform the investment approach.
